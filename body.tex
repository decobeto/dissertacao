\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}
The rising adoption of Industry 4.0 technologies in industrial manufacturing environments has elevated \DTs 
from a theoretical concept explored in academia to an operational necessity. In this context is the concept of a \DT, 
which functions as a virtual replica of a physical asset, continuously updated through automated bidirectional data 
flows \cite{Fuller2020, Kritzinger2018}. \DTs are taking a central position in new-generation intelligent/smart manufacturing, 
which is shifting from knowledge-based intelligent manufacturing to data-driven and knowledge-enable smart manufacturing, in 
which the term ``smart'' refers to the creation and use of data \cite{Tao2019b}. As manufacturing systems gain more complexity, 
the ability to monitor, simulate and reason about physical assets in real time becomes a operational advantage. However, the 
deployment of \DTs in real industrial settings rarely occurs in isolation. As established by the Digital Twin Shop-Floor paradigm 
present by \citeonline{Tao2017ShoopFloor}, true smart manufacturing requires a fundamental transition from isolated asset models to 
fully interconnected ecosystems. A typical shop-floor comprises of multiple heterogeneous assets, each requiring its own \DT 
instance, data pipeline, and lifecycle management. This introduces a class of infrastructure and coordination challenge that 
individual \DT architecture are not designed to address.\\
Through the systematic review presented in Section~\ref{ch:related-work} we show that the existing literature investigates the communication standards, 
composition of \DTs, and internal architectures. Although, the problem of running multiple \DTs instances over a shared infrastructure 
in a scalable, interoperable and operationally continuous way remains not fully addressed.\\
This dissertation addresses this gap by proposing, implementing and evaluating an orchestration architecture for the deployment 
and management of multiple \DT instances in industrial environments. The proposed architecture presented in Section~\ref{ch:proposal} is structured 
across five layers and introduces the concept of the \DTPod as a containerized, self-contained runtime for individual \DT instances. 
The architecture is designed to operate across edge and cloud infrastructure, enforce semantic interoperability between \DT 
instances, and support lifecycle management at a fleet scale.

\section{Problem Context}
\label{ch:problem-context}
Since increasing productivity is always one of the goals of industries, and increasing efficiency, reducing downtime, and having a 
quick response to possible failures are also key objectives, one way to achieve these goals is through Industry 4.0. 
According to \citeonline{Jacoby2020}, the fourth industrial revolution and its digital transformation is revolutionizing industrial 
production as it is about optimization and automation of the previously introduced computerization by intelligent networking of 
machines and processes. Some central elements of this new manufacturing paradigm are the already-existing concepts of 
\IoT, Digital Twins (DT), \AI, \ML, Big Data, Cognitive Services, and their application in industrial production \cite{Jacoby2020}. 
In this context, Digital Twins have emerged as a key enabling technology across multiple industrial domains, like manufacturing, 
aerospace and automotive industries \cite{Kang2024}. \DTs provide the ability to monitor, simulate and predict the behavior of 
already existing physical assets, through the continuously exchange of information between physical and digital counterparts. 
In aerospace industries, as reported by \citeonline{Pierce2023} in the development of NASA's Digital Twin Orion SysML Model, 
\DTs can be used to provide detailed design insight, giving information on the spaceship subsystems to provide adequate standard 
engineering support for flight operations and support real-time flight operations, helping the mission support and the pilot. As 
appointed by \citeonline{Kritzinger2018}, a manufacturing \DT offers an opportunity to simulate and optimize the production system, 
not only enabling a detailed visualization of the manufacturing process from a single component to the whole assembly line but also 
helping with logistical aspects. These use cases share a common characteristic as they do not operate as isolated instances, 
and the operational scale implied by these domains requires a different type of deployment which is the primary motivation for 
this work.\\
The deployment of \DTs at this scale introduces challenges that extend well beyond the design of individual twin instances. 
Provisioning a new twin when an asset is added to the shop-floor, routing sensor data to the correct instance, recovering from pod 
failures, and coordinating model updates without interrupting ongoing operations are tasks that are trackable; however, when 
considered at a large scale, they constitute an orchestration problem that has both industrial and academic dimensions. From an 
industrial perspective, manufacturing environments impose strict operational constraints, such as edge nodes with limited computing 
resources, network reliability, and real-time data processing with latency that can be unfeasible in cloud architectures. From the 
academic perspective, as pointed out by the systematic review present in the Related Work section, while composition, coordination, 
and infrastructure challenges have been investigated, they do not intersect as a scalable, interoperable architecture focused on 
the orchestration of the industrial shop-floor.

\section{Objectives}
\label{ch:objectives}

The objective of this dissertation is to design, implement, and evaluate an orchestration
architecture for deployment and management of multiple Digital Twin instances in industrial
manufacturing environments. As the work aims to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Propose a layered architecture for running and orchestrating digital twins, supporting the full operational lifecycle within the \DT instances over a shared computing infrastructure spanning from edge to cloud.
    \item Design a \DT Pod model that encapsulates the state management and data-driven inference logic of an individual twin as an isolated and containerized unit, along with the 3D simulation tool responsible for demonstrating the machine's operation in real time.
    \item Implement a proof of concept instantiation of the proposed architecture using a representative industrial digital twin, sufficient to validate the orchestration mechanisms under realistic operational conditions.
    \item Evaluate the proposed architecture against the requirements of scalability between instances, and operational continuity during model updates and pod lifecycle events.
\end{itemize}

\section{Digital Twin}
\label{sec:digital_twin}
\subsection{Conceptual Foundations}

The concept of Digital Twin originated from Michael Grieves' work on \PLM 
in the University of Michigan. The original term was ``Conceptual Ideal for \PLM'', later referred to as the
``Mirrored Spaces Model'' and the conceptual model was called ``Information Mirroring Model''  \cite{Grieves2017}.
The term currently used, Digital Twin, was coined at NASA by John Vickers while working with Grieves\cite{Wuni2025}.
One the first use cases of \DTs also occurred at NASA, particularly in the context of the Apollo program, the need to simulate the
effects of the anomaly to understand the problem and find a way to bring the astronauts back safely, through real-time testing to
evaluate solutions, led the engineers in charge to modify the pilot training simulators to replicate the current state of the
lunar module \cite{Allen2021}.\\
Formally, a \DT is defined as a virtual replica of a physical asset, process, or system, being a software composed of models and
services, that intentionally, represent the original system throughout its lifecycle \cite{Dalibor2022}.
\citeonline{Grieves2014}, in his white paper, also describes and proposes a \DT model that forms the basis for all current frameworks,
understanding that \DT is not a unique system but a composite one, this idea is also later developed by \citeonline{Dalibor2022} where they
investigate the conceptual properties and the fundamental concepts that make a \DT. The concept proposed by Grieves divides the
functioning of \DTs into three layers or three main parts: physical entities in real space, virtual models in virtual space, and the
data and information that connects physical entities and virtual models together.\\
Complementing the theoretical definitions, \NIST an agency funded by the
USA government, created guidelines for implementation of \DTs based on the ISO 23247 standard in the
``Advanced Manufacturing Series 400-2 - Use Case Scenarios for Digital Twin Implementation Based on ISO 2324'' written 
by \citeonline{Shao2021}. This technical approach focuses on the standardization of reference architectures, enabling 
context-dependent instantiations, composability, interoperability, and reusability of the \DTs components, 
these standarts would also support other digital twin requirements suas as digital thread, data synchronization, data assurance and
data security, and user interfaces \cite{Shao2021}. \citeonline{Shao2021} and \NIST also address the neeed for integration,
since no single \DT is capable of providing all the solutions for a complex system, equipment, or manufacturing process,
making integration necessary to cover a broader scope. For a set definition a \DT can be understod as an integrated multi-physics,
multi-scale, and probabilistic simulation of a complex product and uses the best available physical models, sensors updates, etc.,
to mirror the life of its corresponding twin \cite{Tao2017PLM}.

\section{Classification}

The concept of Digital Twins in the literature often suffers from a lack of distinction between its representations and
different levels of complexity. Although the concept of \DTs was originally introduced by Grieves in 2002, the technical
differentiation between variations of virtual replicas was consolidated by \citeonline{Kritzinger2018}. 
As proposed by \citeonline{Kritzinger2018}, the differentiation of the digital model occurs through the level of integration of the
data flow between the physical and virtual worlds. The representation of the models is categorized into three different levels:
Digital Twin, Digital Model, and Digital Shadow.\\
The differentiation proposed by \citeonline{Kritzinger2018} establishes an information flow as the dividing line between the
categories. While manual flow characterizes the initial models, increasing automation and the way data is exchanged define the
transition to the terms ``shadow'' and ``digital''. This distinction is illustrated in the communication logic between physical and
virtual assets, as detailed below:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Digital Model: is a digital representation of an existing or planned physical object. This variation does not use any form of automated data exchange between the physical asset and the digital asset. All data exchange is done manually and a change in the state of the physical object has no direct impact or effect on the digital object, in the same way that the digital object does not affect the real one.
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{digitaltwin-Digital Model.drawio.pdf}
        \captionof{figure}{Example of a Digital Model.}
        \label{fig:digital_twin_01}
    \end{center}

    \item Digital Shadow: is a digital representation of an existing asset that has an automatic one-way flow between the physical and digital object, making the digital object interact with the physical one is optional, so that a change in the state of the physical object leads to a change in the digital object only.
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{digitaltwin-Digital shadow.drawio.pdf}
        \captionof{figure}{Example of a Digital Shadow.}
        \label{fig:digital_twin_02}
    \end{center}

    \item Digital Twin: the automatic data flow exists between both the physical and the digital object, making it fully integrated. This way, a change made in the physical object leads to a change in the digital object and vice versa.
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{digitaltwin-Digital Twin.drawio.pdf}
        \captionof{figure}{Example of a Digital Twin.}
        \label{fig:digital_twin_03}
    \end{center}
\end{itemize}

The taxonomy established by \citeonline{Kritzinger2018} provides the necessary conceptual clarity to distinguish static representations
from dynamic \CPS in a definition that can be described as canonical to the field of Digital Twins. However,
the transition from a digital shadow to a full digital twin imposes requirements that transcend simple connectivity.

\section{Internal Reference Architecture and Digital Twin Lifecycle}
\label{sec:internal_reference_and_dt_lifecycle}
Product lifecycle management is the business activity of managing, in the most effective way, the whole lifecycle of products
manufactured by a given company, since their conception and design until their retirement and disposal \cite{Tao2017PLM}.
The digital twin is not one complete model of the physical product, but a set of linked operational data artifacts and
simulation models, which are of suitable granularity for their intended purpose and evolve throughout the product 
lifecycle \cite{Schleich2017}. This idea can explain the concept of digital twins and its applications in different industrial fields,
as the digital must fit its purpose on the use of its physical counterpart. The connection between the physical and the virtual
product enables us to conceptualize, compare, and collaborate \cite{Grieves2014}. This way, the \DT not only serves a 
representation purpose but is also applicable for making predictions about the expected product behaviour, while the granularity 
of the simulation models fits their purpose and evolves from early design stages \cite{Schleich2017}. From the product lifecycle
management point of view, there is a strong need to integrate all lifecycle data artifacts into a comprehensive management system that
can be used by various actors for querying data and making predictions about the physical product from the digital twin for design
optimization and manufacturing \cite{Schleich2017}. To help conceptualize what a \DT is from its design stage to its operation and
insert it into a production lifecycle, \citeonline{Grieves2017} created definitions of how to create and operate \DTs in the triadic
architecture, as can be seen below:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \DTP : Is used to defined the prototypical physical artifact, as the phase used to create and
    design the \DT. It contais the informational sets necessary to describe and produce a physical version taht duplicates or twins
    the virtual version. These informational sets include the requirements to the annotated 3D model, the \BoM,
    \BoP, \BoS and \BoD. This means that in this phase the \DT must have highly
    datailed information about the physical part. The annotated 3D model can be understod as a form that has all the tolerances,
    exact measurements and physical properties. The \BoM is the list of ingredients of the physical part, the \BoP is about the
    instruction manual and the \BoS and \BoD are about repairing and disposing of the physical part, respectively.
    \item \DTI : After being designed and created, instancing the \DT is the next step. The \DTP can be seen as
    the mold, while the \DTI is a digital copy of an individual product operating in the real world. It describes a specific 
    corresponding physical product that an individual Digital Twin remais linked to throughout the life of that physical product. 
    Depending on the use case of this type of Digital Twin, it may contain: the fully annotated 3D model with \GDNT that describes
    the geometry of the physical instance and its components; a \BoP that lists 
    the operations that were performed in creating this physical instance, along with the results of measurements and tests on the 
    instance; a Service Record that describes the past services performed and components replaced; and Operational States captured 
    from actual sensor data, current, past, actual, and future predicted.
    \item \DTE : This is an integrated, multi-domain physical application space for operating on the \DT for 
    a variety of purposes, that can also include prediction, i.e., the Digital Twin would be used for predicting future behavior and 
    performance of the physical product. In the prototyping stage, this would be the prediction of the behavior of the designed 
    product with components that vary between its high and low tolerances in order to ascertain that the as-designed product met the 
    proposed requirements. In the instance stage the prediction would be specific to the physical counterpart that can incorporate 
    actual data to the mixture. The predicted performance can be based on the current product lifecycle at its current state and can 
    use it to move forward in the simulations. The variety of purposes can also be interrogative, which apply to the \DTIs that can be 
    interrogated for current and past histories, without caring of where their physical counterpart resideds in the world. Individual 
    instances can be interrogated for their current system state, as temperature or fuel level as example, while multiple instances 
    of products would provide data that could be correlated for predicting future states, like correlating readings of multiple 
    sensors with subsequent failure, for example. This aggregation can be used to provide Bayesian probabilities for predictive uses.
\end{itemize}

According to \citeonline{Schleich2017} has been reported in the scientific literature and in practice that the current limitations to the 
implementation of the vision of the \DTs are insufficient for synchronization between the physical and the digital world to establish 
closed loops, the missing of high-fidelity models for simulation and virtual testing at multiple scales, the lacking uncertainty 
quantification for such models, the difficulties in the prediction of complex systems, as the challenges for gathering and processing 
large data sets. The advances in information technology are driving the manufacturing industry toward the big data era, and 
data analysis and mining are gradually playing a more significant role in manufacturing enterprise mangement. Big data analytics 
can provide systematic guidance for related production activities through effectively collecting and analyzing a variety of data 
generated in the entire product lifecycle \cite{Tao2017PLM}. In this way, collecting and analyzing a large volume of manufacturing 
data to find patterns, relationships, or regularities that govern the behavior of the system and knowledge, about decison making in 
manufacturing, has become the key to smart manufacturing \cite{Qi2018}. In this context, and with the aim of addressing contemporary 
challenges related to Big Data, data integration, and the massive collection of information from sensors, \citeonline{Tao2018} proposes 
an extension to the triadic model originally presented by Michael Grieves. The author expands the architecture to a five-dimensional 
model, incorporating data and services layers. According to \citeonline{Qi2021}, the 5D model can be formally defined by the following 
expression:

\[DT = (PE, VM, Ss, DD, CN)\]

Where each one of this componentes plays a critical role in the system architecture, and can be explained as follows as 
reported by \citeonline{Qi2021}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \PE: The physical world is the foundation of the \DT, an it may consist of device or product, physical system, activities process, and even an organization. It's implemented based on the physical laws and deals with the uncertainty of the environment, the \PE can be encompassed in a diverse spectrum where it can range from individual devices to a complex operational process. These \PEs can be divided into three levels according to function and structure, which are: the unit level, which focuses on individual components of machines representing a lesser scale of modeling; the system level, which can be understood as the coordination of multiple units and it complex functions; and the \SoS level, that can be the highest level of abstraction, where independent and heterogeneous systems collaborate to achieve objectives, such as an industrial plant or logistics companies. The \PEs can be understood as the entire foundation for the digital architecture construction.
    \item \VM: Virtual models must be a faithful replica of the physical entities, which reproduce the physical geometries, properties, behaviors and rules. It can be divided into four different models that can be understood as the 3-dimensional geometric models that describe a physical entity in terms of its shape, size, tolerance and structural relation. The Physics Models reflect the physical phenomena of the entities based on their physical properties, such as deformation, delamination, fracture and corrosion. Behavior model describe the behaviors, which are the transition of states, and responding mechanisms of the entities against changes in the external environment. The rule models equip \DTs with logical abilities such as reasoning, judgment, evaluation and autonomous decision-making, that works based upon the rules extracted from historical data or come from domain experts.
    \item \Ss: Services are essential components of \DTs mainly in today's extremely connected world and in the light of the \XaaS paradigm. The operation of a \DT requires the continuous support of various platform services, which accommodate the customized software development, model building, and service delivery. It can be understood in two different contexts. Firstly, the \DT provides users with application services concerning simulation, verification, monitoring, etc. Secondly, a number of third-party services are needed in the process of building a functioning \DT, such as data services, knowledge services, algorithms services, and so on.
    \item \DD: The data is the key driver of a \DT, as it deals with multi-temporal scale, multi-dimension, multi-source, and heterogeneous data. Some of this data is obtained from \PEs, including static attribute data and dynamic condition data. Some data is generated by virtual models, which can reflect in the simulation result. Some data is obtained from services, which describe the service invocation and execution, or can be provided by domain experts or extracted from existing data, as it can be understood as knowledge. There is also fusion data, which is generated as the results of the combined data mentioned above.
    \item \CN: The digital representation is connected dynamically with their real counterpart to enable advanced simulation, operation, and analysis. This data exchange occurs with the connections between \PEs, virtual models, and services. There are 6 connections for a \DT, which are the connection between the physical asset and its digital model, between the physical assets and data, between physical asset and services, between the digital model and data,  between the digital model and services, and between services and data. This way, all the parts of what makes a \DT can collaborate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Interoperability}
\label{sec:interoperability}
%%\subsubsection{Definition of Interoperability}
According to the ISO/IEC 25010 standard, interoperability can be defined as: \textit{"degree to which two or more systems, products or components can exchange information and use the information that has been exchanged"} \cite{David2024}, 
which is fundamental for enabling complex and heterogeneous systems, devices, and applications to exchange information and
operate cooperatively. This concept is relevant in distributed cyber-physical environments, where systems and machines developed by
different vendors and based on different technologies must interact seamlessly.\\
As interoperability is a key requirement of Industry 4.0 \cite{Jacoby2020}, which is essential for enabling the integration of
multiple software platforms, sensors, simulation systems, and decision making tools. \citeonline{Vernadat2010} emphasizes that
interoperability includes the ability of exchanging or sharing information and using functionality of one another in a distributed 
and heterogeneous environment, not being limited to the technical exchange of data but encompassing also to its interpretation. 
For computer science and software engineering, interoperability is a question of levels of compatibility in terms of communication 
protocols, communication interfaces, data accesses, data types, data semantics, application functionality and dynamic behavior while 
integration is a matter of \DB schemas and information systems integration \cite{Vernadat2010}.\\
As digital infrastructures become increasingly interconnected, interoperability stands out as a key enabler for the integration of 
\CPS, allowing companies and organizations to combine multiple technological components into operational 
environments or platforms.

\subsection{Levels of Interoperability}
\label{sec:levels_of_interoperability}
In technical systems, multiple levels of interoperability must be considered \cite{Jacoby2020}, reflecting the increasing complexity
of system integration. Reference frameworks are useful instruments to position and relate to one another, but also to compare, 
concepts, principles, methods, standards, models and tools in a certain domain of concern \cite{Vernadat2010}. 
According to \citeonline{Vernadat2010} \EC in the \EIF defines three 
essential levels of interoperability, namely technical, semantic and organizational. The technical level is about facilitating 
communication and interchange in terms of communication protocols, data exchange and message passing among application protocols. 
The semantic level can be defined as the ability to share, aggregate or synchronize data/information across heterogeneous information 
systems, ensuring that such communicating systems can interpret common and shared information in a consistent way. The organizational 
level is responsible with business goals addressing the requirements of user community, aligning and coordinating business processes 
and bringing collaboration capabilities to organizations that wish to exchange information. Depending on the author, variations can 
be found, as cited by \citeonline{Jacoby2020}, there are multiple levels of interoperability, so it is also possible to find the 
three levels as: technical interoperability, which enables data exchange on the physical level; syntactic interoperability, 
where a system can parse the received data; and semantic interoperability, which refers to the ability of computer systems to 
exchange data with unambiguous shared meaning.\\
While the classification of interoperability into technical, semantic, and organizational levels provides a useful abstraction for 
understanding system integration, it may not fully capture the complexity of interactions required in advanced distributed systems. 
In particular, environments like the Digital Twin ecosystem that demands deeper levels of interoperability, including shared 
understanding of context, behavior, and system dynamics.\\
To address these limitations, the \LCIM, proposed by \citeonline{Tolk2003}, extends the 
traditional interoperability classifications by introducing additional levels that consider not only data exchange and meaning but 
also the use, interpretation, and dynamic behavior of information within systems. As explained by \citeonline{Wang2009} the \LCIM 
defines seven interoperability levels. In the first level, L0, there is no interoperability between systems. At the L1 (Technical) 
level there is a technical connection between systems and can exchange data between them. At the L2 (Syntatic) level, system have a 
protocol that is agreed upon to exchange right forms of data in the right order, but the meaning/semantic of the data is not 
established. At the L3 (Semantic) level interoperating systems are exchanging a set of terms that they can semantically 
parse/interpret. At the L4 (Pragmatic) level, the interoperating systems will be aware of the context, like system states and 
processes, and the meaning of information being exchanged. At the L5 (Dynamic) level, interoperating systems are able to re-orient 
information production and consumption based on understood changes to meaning, due to the change of context as time increases. 
At the last level L6 (Conceptual), interoperating systems are fully aware of each other´s information, processes, contexts, and 
modeling assumptions. According to \citeonline{David2024} the most important transitions in interoperability levels for the digital 
twin context are the L3, L4, L5 and L6. These additional levels are particularly relevant in \DT systems where multiple interacting 
twins and machines must not only exchange data but also coordinate behaviors, share contextual information and operate consistently 
across distributed environments.

\subsection{Interoperability in Digital Twin Systems}
Interoperability is particularly important in the context of of Digital Twin systems, where multiple technological components must 
interact to maintain a synchronized relationship between physical assets and their virtual counterparts. \DT environments usually 
integrate sensors, data acquisition systems, simulation models, analytics platforms and visualization tools. Nonetheless, integrating 
\DTs into complex cyber-physical ecosystem can raise some interoperability challenges. As stated by \citeonline{Acharya2026}, some 
works report latency, platform mismatches, inconsistent data standards, organizational collaboration, knowledge exchange, limited 
scalability, non-modular architectures, legacy systems, and many more as being interoperability-driven challenges.\\
To address these challenges, some enabling technologies and industrial standards have been developed to support interoperability in 
Digital Twin environments. According to \citeonline{Jacoby2020}, achieving interoperability on a global scale requires formal 
standardization. Most organizations rely on open standards as \textit{\OPCUA}, 
\textit{\MQTT}, \REST \APIs, regulatory guidelines like ISO/IEC/SAREF and structured 
representation of \DTs like \AAS (Asset Administration Shell), created by the collective work of several german institutions including 
the industrial associations ZVEI, VDMA, BITKOM and researches from the VDI/VDE \cite{Adolphs2015}, as necessary enablers for complex 
integrations \cite{Acharya2026}. At the communication level, protocols such as \textit{\OPCUA} provide a plataform-independent 
mechanism for data exchange between industrial devices and software systems, supporting both syntatic and semantic interoperability. 
In addition, the concept of the \textit{\AAS} has emerged as a key element for interoperability in the Industry 4.0, which can be 
understood as a standardized digital representation of an asset, providing a common interface and a data model.\\
Beyond these standards, interoperability in Digital Twin systems is also supported by modern software and data technologies, 
including cloud computing platforms, microservices architectures, and data integration frameworks. The integration of multiple 
Digital Twins requires not only standardized communication protocols but also shared data models and semantic communication across 
systems. In this context, interoperability can be considered a key enabling factor for the development of scalable and distributed 
Digital Twin architectures, as interoperability is the key requirement factor of Industry 4.0 \cite{Jacoby2020}, supporting the 
coordination and interaction of multiple digital models across heterogeneous technological platforms.

\section{Edge Computing}
\label{sec:edge_computing}
\citeonline{Kong2022} define edge computing as a distributed computing architecture that extends cloud computing to the edge of the network,
close to the data source. The edge is considered a feasible computing basis for offloading computing tasks in the \IoT, as it can provide real-time data analysis, increase network capacity, and avoid data transmission congestion. In addition, 
it avoids the high latency and bandwidth required for processing data in the cloud.\\
As explained by \cite{Picone2023}, \DTs cannot be delegated only to the cloud but should instead be handled as close as possible to 
the physical assets in order to maximize their efficiency. Latency and reliability issues introduce disruption to \DTs since the link 
between the physical and the logical object is effective only if the refresh time is lower than the average access time of the 
application using the twin. Edge computing paradigms allow overcoming these limitations, enabling \DTs to operate with ultra-responsive 
and ultra-reliable connectivity in multiple operational contexts \cite{Picone2023}.

\section{Orchestration}
\label{sec:orchestration}
In the context of this work, orchestration can be understood as the coordinated execution of multiple computer systems,
applications, and services to ensure that deployment, configuration, and other processes are performed in the proper sequence.
Modern orchestration technologies encompass a wide spectrum of tools and platforms, each addressing specific aspects of service
coordination, with focus on declarative configuration, infrastructure as code, and automated deployment pipelines \cite{Singhal2024}.\\
As defined by \citeonline{Nguyen2024} digital twin orchestration is the process of managing and coordinating the interactions
between multiple digital twins that represent physical assets, systems, or processes. This allows for the creation of complex
simulations, scenarios, and optimizations that can improve the performance, efficiency, and resilience of the physical counterparts
and also facilitate the integration of data, analytics, and artificial intelligence across different domains and industries.

\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:related-work}
The orchestration and interoperability of multiple \DTs have become a key issue in the design of industrial \DT ecosystems, 
which led to the rise of a number of architectural proposals based on solving common issues: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item How \DTs can be coordinated during runtime?
    \item What kind of architecture and infrastructure should underpin the operation and deploy of several \DTs at a scale?
    \item How \DTs can be composed using the \DTs of their components? 
\end{itemize} 
The area of coordination and orchestration is unified in terms of recurring theme of an orchestration layer or agent facilitating 
communications between the peer components of \DTs. For example, \cite{Monsalve2021} uses an architecture of \MAS, 
these agents cooperate with each other to solve problems and make decisions, they work as a way to give decentralized intelligence 
to the \DTs, in the domain of power systems. The system utilizes \DTCs, which are containerized agents that are 
automated and provide presentation of grid and power components, and a \DTM, which is an orchestration component 
that works controlling the \DTCs. \cite{Xu2024} have proposed an approach to the \CDT consisting of using 
multi-layered designs to allow interaction between \DTs in physical and virtual environments. The authors also use a \MAS approach as 
a way to organize the production integration the \DT models to the \MAS agents, the authors divide the agents into three categories, 
the task agents, workstation agents and the robot agents. \cite{Nguyen2024} also proposes a multi-layered design, an orchestration 
framework structured in four functional layers: federation, translation, brokering and synchronization. This allows for efficient 
coordination between \DT models. \cite{Nie2023} proposes the combination \DTs and \MAS in cloud-edge orchestration, organizing 
geographically distributed manufacturing resources. \cite{Pan2025} presents a \DTN architecture functioning 
as an orchestration space for several connected \DT models and managing their lifecycle. \cite{Alhazmi2025} study \DTNs too, 
introducing five layers in their multi-layer architecture for efficient data dissemination of \DTs based on \MQTT.\\
Interoperability and data heterogeneity represent a persistent obstacle across these orchestration approaches. \cite{Juhlin2025} 
handle this with the CRADLE architecture (short for Cross-Vendor Asset Digital Twin Architecture), an interoperable and modular \DT 
framework that combines multiple interoperability standards to supper cross-vendor \DT integration, and used a paper machine to do a 
proof of concept. \cite{Wang2025} address semantic heterogeneity through an ontology-based fusion model, allowing \DTs from different 
domains to exchange information between themselves in a semantically consistent manner. \cite{Ali2025} approaches the cross-domain 
interoperability through a blockchain-based framework, with a focus on security in IoT-driven manufacturing environments.\\
At the level of infrastructure, a related research line looks into the provision, lifecycle management, and scalability issues of 
the \DTs ecosystems. In \cite{Talasila2023}, the concept of \DTaaS is introduced as a centralized platform 
for creation and lifecycle management of \DTs in a standardized way. \cite{Cakir2025} builds on \DTaaS by introducing the \IoTw approach and proposing a Digital-Twin-as-a-Platform (DTaaP), stating that scalability is achieved by disaggregation of 
\DT functionalities within the distributed cloud architecture. \cite{Frasheri2025} contributes another piece to \DT infrastructure by 
suggesting a federated \DTaaS architecture, aligning the need for distributed orchestration of \DTs with a federated \DTaaS deployment 
architecture. \cite{Merkle2019} introduces a control master handler for managing a fleet of entities within cloud middleware. 
\cite{Qian2024} suggest using a distributed data layer as a solution to large-scale data input issue coming from multiple \CPS, 
building hierarchical architecture for synthesizing \DT techniques as a separate layer within their architecture. \cite{Picone2023} 
provides a distributed microservice architecture to deploy multiple \EDTs within the locality, among the edge 
nodes with multiple \DTs, hence bringing the \DTs closer to their physical counterparts. The problems of centralized scalability are 
addressed by \cite{Tripathy2024} by means of the decentralized edge approach via the Eclipse Arrowhead Framework, which results in 
the industrial scenario being set up in the form of a \SoS. The work of \cite{Maree2024} is dedicated to the issue of 
\DaaS for \IoT devices and presents the concept of provisioning service as the stateful service managing the 
application and updating multiple \DTIs. Federated models are extended by \cite{Martella2025}, proposing 
federated \DTs as a way to manage distributed data in a \DTE.\\
In relation to the composition, various research studies have investigated how complex \DTs can be built from smaller \DTs. 
For example, \cite{Schroeder2021} developed a model of ``Aggregate \DTs'' based on a bottom-up architectural approach. 
Meanwhile, \cite{Mahoro2023} introduced ``\CDTs'' which are created using \ADTs. 
Following the above research, \cite{Kuruppuarachchi2023} extended the \CDT model to solve interoperability issues within \DT silos 
using a \DTConnector to provide an interface between the individual twins. The same can be applied 
to \cite{Talasila2025}, who used a compositional \DT strategy, thus allowing for modular independent digital twins to form 
representation of larger-scale systems. \cite{Lai2024} used a  multi-layered architecture for the topic of smart cities, which 
supports different \DTs to communicate and share their what-if simulations. This demonstrates that the composition pattern can be 
used outside of the shop-floor of an industry. Another similar problem is solved by the idea of Digital Twin Continuum applied 
by \cite{Barbone2024}, coordinating multiples \DTs across edge-to-cloud environments.\\
Several problems, though, can be identified in the described solutions. Most architectural models deal with composition, 
orchestration, or infrastructure individually but lack a coherent architectural approach to address the issues altogether. 
The interoperability schemes suggested by many researchers are often limited by either the application context in which they 
operate or even by the underlying communication protocols used in the system being developed. Finally, there is insufficient 
scientific exploration regarding multiple \DT orchestration in industrial shop-floor environments that consider scalability and 
interoperability as design requirements from the beginning. The architecture proposed in this dissertation aims to fill this gap. 
The table containing the articles mentioned and analyzed in this section can be found in the~\autoref{apx:systematic_literature_review_table}.

\chapter{Proposal}
\label{ch:proposal}

The deployment of Digital Twins in industrial manufacturing environments does not occur in isolation. \DTs are fully integrated with 
the shop floor, which is composed of multiple heterogeneous assets, as machines, conveyors, robots and sensors, each potentially 
requiring its own DT instance, its own data pipeline, and its own lifecycle management.


Taking these considerations into account, for the purpose of this work, a Digital Twin Ecosystem is understood as a networked 
arrangement of \DTs operating over a shared platform, in which semantic interoperability enables fleet-level reasoning and knowledge 
transfer across different assets, subject to the data governance constraints of the deployment context. This definition can be 
considered an expansion of the Digital Twin Environment introduced by \citeonline{Grieves2017} and described in Section~\ref{sec:internal_reference_and_dt_lifecycle}, 
extended to accommodate fleet-scale platforms in the industrial context. The central argument of this proposal is that realizing 
such an ecosystem in practice requires a dedicated orchestration architecture, one that can automate digital twin lifecycle 
management, enforce the isolation between tenants, and distribute computation between edge and cloud according to latency and data 
sovereignty constraints. The following sections present and describe the proposed architecture in detail.

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
The proposed architecture  addresses the operational challenges of managing multiple concurrent digital twin instances in a 
heterogeneous edge-cloud industrial environment, providing mechanisms for automated provisioning, data routing, model deployment and 
lifecycle management. As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:digital_twin_01}, the architecture is composed by five layers: the physical edge, 
data ingestion, storage, execution and application services with data analytics.

\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{DTaaS_architecture.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Proposed architecture for multiple digital twin hosting and orchestration}
    \label{fig:dtaas_architecture}
\end{center}

The technologies selected for the architectural representation were not chosen and are used only as examples of how the layers work. 
The five layers and their operational methods are detailed below.

\subsection{Physical Edge Layer}
The physical edge layer is the interface between the industrial shop floor and the digital platform. It comprises the physical assets, machines, and sensors that generate the data consumed by the platform, as well as the gateway infrastructure responsible for collecting, pre-processing, and forwarding it to the ingestion layer.\\
The communication between the gateway and the industrial assets can be made using the \OPCUA protocol, as the \OPCUA provides a standardized framework for secure, reliable, and platform-independent communication that allows digital twins to integrate with diverse systems and components \cite{David2024}, this way allowing the gateway to consume data from heterogeneous machines without requiring custom-made integration for each asset type.\\
The gateway pre-processes the raw data before sending it upstream. These way, it is possible to guarantee data validation and timestamp alignment, ensuring the data delivered to the ingestion layer is consistent in format regardless of the source asset. Is also important that the gateway provides a buffering capability, allowing it to retain data locally during periods of connectivity interruption and sending it once the connection is restored. This is particularly relevant in industrial environments, where networks reliability cannot always be guaranteed.\\
The connection between the gateway and the ingestion layer is established via lightweight messaging protocol. The specific protocol, \MQTT or WebSocket, will be selected during the implementation phase based on empirical evaluation of the latency and throughput characteristics of the monitored assets, as both are viable candidates for this role. This decision is considered an implementation detail and does not affect the architectural design of either the edge layer or the data ingestion layer.

\subsection{Data Ingestion Layer}

The data ingestion layer is responsible for receiving telemetry and data streams from the physical edge layer, validating their structure, and routing each message to the correct processing destination. This layer is built based on a central message broker, which is complemented by a schema registry and the event router so that governance can be assured. \\
The message broker is the central intake point for all the data and telemetry entering the platform. It receives data streams from multiples assets concurrently and decouples it, to the \DTPods through the schema registry and message broker and also sends the data to the storage. Each asset is identified by a unique identifier embedded in every message it produces, allowing the broker to organize incoming data by source and maintain message ordering within each asset stream, and ensuring that each \DTPod consumes only the data to its physical counterpart.\\
The schema registry will be used to enforce a data contract between the machines and the platform as a way to validate whether the data is coming from the right asset. As updates in the machine software or firmware can change the names used in fields or other data sensors, this mechanism can be used to alert the operator to prevent data corruption or other problems, and to maintain syntactic interoperability as cited in the LCIM Framework at Section~\ref{sec:levels_of_interoperability}, ensuring that the data used in the platform is structurally consistent.\\
The event router is responsible for consuming the messages from the message broker and forwarding them to the right \DTPod instance based on the asset identifier embedded in the message payload. When a new DT instance is provisioned, the twin manager registers the new asset identifier within the event router, which begins to send the corresponding data stream to the pod. When the pod is decommissioned, the registration can be removed and the data stream is either archived or redirected.

\subsection{Storage Layer}

The storage layer provides persistent data management across three distinct stores, each optimized for a different category of data generated and consumed by the platform. The separation into specialized stores reflects the fundamentally different access patterns, retention requirements, and query characteristics of each data type making use of deeper \DB concepts with polyglot persistence.\\
The first storage is a time-series \DB, which receives continuous telemetry streams produced by the physical assets, such as sensor readings, temperature measurements, vibration data and other operational parameters, and saves the information according to each asset to which that data belongs. The time-series store is the primary data source for the analytics within the Application layer, which can use the historical data to train anomaly detection and predictive maintenance.\\
The second storage is an object store that employs \SthreeStorage compatible storage, which is used to store binary artifacts that have a lifecycle independent of the operational data, like the 3D model of the physical assets.\\
The third storage is a graph \DB, which can be used to maintain the ontological model of the machines, their components, the sensors attached to them, and the relationships between the entities. This knowledge graph can be used for the semantic enrichment of anomaly detection and other patterns that can be discovered through data analysis. For example, when a \DTPod detects an anomalous reading from a sensor, the graph can be queried to determine which component the sensor monitors and what can be expected if failures occur. This contextual information can help with maintenance and other recommendations.

\subsection{Execution Layer}
\label{sec:executin_layer}
The execution layer is the operational core of the proposed platform and the primary focus of this work. It will be implemented using a container orchestration platform, possibly Kubernetes, which can provide the container scheduling, health monitoring, and lifecycle management capabilities necessary to operate the concurrent \DTPods instances over the shared computing infrastructure.\\
This layer can be divided into two functional components: the Stream Processor and the \DTPod instances. The Stream Processor performs the streaming and processing of the data arriving from the ingestion layer before it can be delivered to individual pods. The Stream Processor acts as an intermediary that ensures each \DTPod receives preprocessed and contextually enriched data, which can be obtained from the knowledge graph, rather than raw data.\\
Each \DTPod is a containerized unit that encapsulates the complete runtime of a single DT instance. The \DTPod maintains an internal Model State, which represents the current operational state of the physical asset, continuously updated as new data arrives from the Stream Processor. One possible way to achieve this is to periodically checkpoint the Model State to the object store to help with eventual pod restarts and ensure that the DT is preserved across infrastructure events without the need to re-activate the physical asset. The processing logic applied to the Model State, which can be a hybrid approach between a physics-based simulation and a data-driven inference model, is encapsulated within the pod. This design decision treats the Digital Twin as a hot-reload mechanism that can be changed without affecting different parts of the architecture, like new versions of the physical asset can be loaded into the object store.\\
The \DTPod produces output results and recommended actions to be taken on the physical machine. This results can be used in the Application Services layer for visualization and analytics, and routed back to the physical asset, providing a closed-loop and staying within the current propositions of what it means to be a digital twin, as proposed by \cite{Kritzinger2018}. The orchestration layer that exists between the Execution layer and the Twin Manager directs the full lifecycle of the \DTPod instances; when a new asset is activated in the platform, the twin manager submits a pod specification to the container orchestration platform, to make it available on the compute node. When an asset is decommissioned, the Twin Manager instructs the container orchestrator to terminate the corresponding pod and archive its final state checkpoint.

\subsection{Application Services Layer}

The Application Services layer provides the interfaces through which operators, analysts, and external systems interact with the platform. The access to the data is mediated by an \API Gateway, which enforces authentication, rate limiting, and routing across the layer's services.\\
The Dashboard service provides real-time operational visibility into the fleet of monitored assets, consuming the data from each \DTPod, and also presenting performance indicators, anomaly alerts and fleet status to the operators at the shop floor. The dashboard is updated continuously as new data arrives from the execution layer, providing a live view of operational status of all machines, also providing analytics services on a fleet-level, without the need to interact with individual \DTs instances directly.\\
The 3D visualization service can be implemented using a range of technologies, with the main candidate being the Unity game engine, which can be used to render interactive three dimensional models of each monitored asset and animating it in response to the current model state. This allows the operators and other users to intuitively interpret the machine's condition through its digital representation. The 3D model assets are loaded from the object store at initialization, making the visualization service purely a consumer of the state produced by the execution layer.
As defined in Section~\ref{sec:executin_layer}, the twin manager acts as the control panel to the operational lifecycle of all the \DTs on the platform. It shows a interface in which operators can provision new \DTs, monitor the status of running pods and decommission running \DTs. 

\section{Methodology and Timeline}
\label{sec:methodology_and_timeline}
This research follows an applied investigation grounded in the \DSR methodology as presented by \citeonline{Hevner2004}, 
as it seeks to address practical and domain-specific problems in the industrial context, as it follows the absence of a structured 
architecture for the orchestration of multiple concurrent Digital Twin instances. With this, the aim of this research work  is to 
generate knowledge through direct applicability, culminating in the development of a technological artifact that is relevant to the 
advancement of Industry 4.0.\\
To achieve the stated objectives, an exploratory approach is adopted, systematically investigating the gaps identified in the state 
of the art to propose a solution within a complex and evolving technological domain. The research design combines qualitative and 
quantitative methods, as the nature of the problem requires both conceptual rigor in the architecture design phase and empirical 
validation in the evaluation phase.\\
The qualitative component manifests in the systematic literature review, as the elicitation of architectural requirements, and the 
conceptual modeling of the proposed architecture. This phase establishes the theoretical knowledge foundations upon which the 
architecture is constructed, ensuring that the proposed solution is grounded in the current state of the art and addresses the 
existing gaps in the literature.\\
The quantitative component is applied in the validation phase, as the architecture is implemented together with the test bed \DT, 
where the empirical data is collected and analyzed, to evaluate its performance. The evaluation metrics include latency, 
resource utilization, and system throughput under representative operational conditions, measured through controlled benchmarks. 
This empirical evaluation is intended to provide evidence of the solution's efficiency and scalability, enabling an evidence-based 
assessment of the proposed architecture's suitability for real-world industrial deployments, after this stage the methodology and 
result chapters will be written. The dissertation execution plan is presented below in the~\autoref{fig:gantt}.
